# Space and documentary craft

Contents: what top channels do, scale and numbers, analogies, named actors, generous assumption, writing for the ear, fact handling and uncertainty, AV notes.

Labels: OFFICIAL, CREATOR, OBSERVED (read in the eight reference transcripts), INFERENCE (reasoned, untested). Apply INFERENCE items as defaults and keep them tunable.

## What the best science channels demonstrably do

Primary-source research, many drafts and cuts, an opening question or concrete image instead of a thesis, delayed payoff, plain words, voice or script locked before visuals are timed, and a second reader who checks for confusion (CREATOR: Kurzgesagt keeps a sourcesheet per video, writes about a dozen drafts and records the voiceover first; Vox's black-hole video team used script editors to catch confusion the author could not see). The Vox black-hole video also made scale concrete with real imagery, delayed the reveal until viewers grasped the difficulty, kept narration plain because viewers process picture, music and voice at once, and built pauses for big ideas. Its maker names the main flaw: the script set up an expectation the facts did not match. Do not script an expectation before the results are known.

## Scale ladder (OBSERVED, Kurzgesagt and a Hindi black-hole video)

Climb in rungs, one number each, each rung bigger than the last, with the same anchor ("aap") travelling through. Example order: Earth, Moon, solar system, nearest star, galaxy, local group, supercluster. Name each rung, give one number, move on. Pause after the biggest.

Human-scale conversions (CREATOR guidance plus OBSERVED): tie each big number to one comparison the viewer can picture, shown on screen as it is spoken.

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| One anchor per number | One human-scale comparison, shown on screen as it is said |
| Distance as travel time | "How long a train or plane would take" beats raw kilometres |
| Age as compression | Compress cosmic time to a year or a day |
| Round for the ear | Say "lagbhag chaar lakh kilometre"; keep the exact figure as an on-screen callout |
| Define once, reuse | Define each term in plain words at first use, then use the same word every time |
| Local anchors | Indian cities, train speeds, cricket pitch or boundary, lakh and crore. Choose for accuracy first; untested but likely to land |
| Guess first | Ask the viewer to predict, pause, then reveal |

Say a line that names the problem when it helps: "very far" and "a million times very far" feel the same to our brains. The conversions then solve it.

## Analogies

- One analogy per hard idea, and build your own from the topic. Do not reuse the reference videos' analogies.
- Say where the analogy stops being true. A Hindi black-hole video does this ("real spacetime is not a rubber sheet, but it gives a good idea").
- Prefer everyday objects the viewer has handled (a thrown stone, a drain, a siren) over other abstractions.
- Define a term first, then show the analogy, then use the term again.

## Named actor (OBSERVED)

Attach abstract physics to someone doing something: the viewer as a traveller, a named fictional civilisation, a crew with an arrival message, a scientist with a deadline. Four of four Kurzgesagt videos carried the idea through an imagined actor or tour. A human story or one person on screen also makes abstractions easier to feel (CREATOR, Heath and Heath).

## Generous assumption, then collapse (OBSERVED)

Grant the most optimistic assumption openly ("let's pretend our ship goes at one fifth the speed of light"), then show why it still fails. Or state the loophole and give a whole chapter to whether it really works, ending with the limits. The viewer expects rescue and learns why not. Always state the assumption aloud so the argument stays honest.

## Solve, then new problem (OBSERVED)

"Great, next stop is..." then "Oh. Oh no." Each section ends on a fresh obstacle. This builds a But-Therefore chain that carries 10 to 15 minutes.

## Deflation (OBSERVED, translate by function)

After an awe moment, a flat, short, slightly rude line ("lame and depressing", "oh well"). The job is to let the viewer breathe and to signal that the narrator is not overselling. Do not translate English jokes word for word; write a Hinglish line that does the same job. Use sparingly, a few per video, not a joke after every fact.

## Writing for the ear

- One idea per sentence, the key word near the end, contractions and plain forms.
- Read aloud at pace, including planned pauses. Mark every stumble and every line you would not say to a friend.
- Median sentence about 12 words; a long sentence only after a build; punch beats of one to four words after a long build ("Oh." "Everywhere." "Forever.") (OBSERVED).
- Numbers: write them as they should be spoken. Round for the ear and show the exact figure on screen.
- Names: keep a pronunciation list for recurring planets, missions and scientists. Voice tools misread acronyms; see `hinglish-voice.md`.

## Fact handling and uncertainty

Source hierarchy (INFERENCE): mission data and the underlying paper first (NASA, ESA, ISRO, JAXA), then preprints flagged as not peer reviewed, then expert and university press offices, then quality science journalism, then encyclopedias for orientation only.

Space-specific hazards: revised numbers (distances, ages, exoplanet counts, mission dates), tentative "habitable" or "life found" claims, artist's concepts mistaken for photographs, and false-colour images such as JWST and black-hole images. Flag these aloud or on screen. Label fabricated visuals of real objects "artist's concept" or "illustration".

Uncertainty (UBC guidance, OFFICIAL-style): use calibrated hedges, attach figures instead of vague qualifiers, present estimates as estimates, say when more information is expected. One hedge per claim. Mark speculation before it starts ("yeh andaza hai, pakka nahi") and again where evidence ends. After a dramatic alarm hook, say plainly what is not known. Three of four reference videos did this (OBSERVED). Do not overstate ignorance ("scientists ko kuch nahi pata") when leading explanations exist.

Keep an "as of" date on fast-changing facts and recheck them before publishing. Agency media reuse terms were not verified; check per agency, and credit when required.

## AV notes (kept light)

This skill writes narration. Optional `> VISUAL:` notes may say what the picture should show (shot type or source) in a few words, and mark real-footage lines separately from generated ones so credits are tracked. Writing full image or video prompts belongs to the prompt skill. Good scripts for video are written for video, not adapted from articles, and a story should become a video only if it has distinctly visual elements (CREATOR, Vox alumni).
