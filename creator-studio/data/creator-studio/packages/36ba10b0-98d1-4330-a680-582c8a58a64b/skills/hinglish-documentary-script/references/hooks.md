# Hooks

Contents: anatomy, the alignment rule, hook types, template library, Hinglish examples, timeline, mistakes.

Evidence labels used in this skill: OFFICIAL (YouTube's own guidance), EXPERIMENT (controlled study, small samples), DATA (measured numbers from one creator or vendor), CREATOR (a named practitioner's own method), OBSERVED (read or counted in the eight reference transcripts), INFERENCE (reasoned, untested). Treat INFERENCE as a default to test, not a rule.

## Anatomy of a hook

The hook is not a preamble. It is the first slice of the real content, already carrying the question. In one creator's 63-graph analysis, getting to content within 10 to 20 seconds with no engineered intro scored best, while introducing yourself with credentials scored worst (DATA, single source, tutorial niche). YouTube says the 30-second "intro" rate shows whether the opening matched what the title and thumbnail promised (OFFICIAL).

The shape seen in three of the four Kurzgesagt hooks, in 44 to 72 seconds, and echoed in the Hindi videos (OBSERVED):

1. A flat bold claim or a concrete scene. No greeting.
2. A stake or an emotion word ("lonely", "lame and depressing", "dangerous").
3. A turn: "but", "lekin", "par ek catch hai".
4. One specific number or a teaser fact.
5. An explicit promise or invitation: what this video will resolve, not how.
6. Then the first chapter card or title.

Hindi long-form videos often delay the explicit promise to 2 to 3 minutes but give one complete small insight inside the first minute to earn patience (OBSERVED, 2 of 3). For a short script of 6 to 8 minutes, use the 45 to 75 second form.

Hook length in words at 2.73 words per second: 45 s is about 123 words, 60 s about 164, 75 s about 205.

## The alignment rule (INFERENCE built on OFFICIAL guidance)

Title is the question or claim, thumbnail is the strongest image of it, and the hook is the first answer to "yes, this is that, and here is why it is worth staying." The first frame should match the thumbnail. The first line should echo the title without repeating it verbatim (verbatim repetition scored 52 to 59 percent at 30 seconds in the 63-graph data). Raise the question the packaging implies. Do not answer it in the hook and do not swap in an easier one. If the title makes a specific promise, deliver the first evidence of it inside the hook.

For a Hinglish channel: packaging language should match narration language. In that same data, non-English videos with English packaging lost viewers when native speech began (DATA, single source).

## Hook types and when each fits (INFERENCE)

| Hook type | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Curiosity gap or puzzle | Explainers where the answer is the product | Opening a gap the viewer has no context for |
| Misconception first | Space and physics with popular wrong intuitions | Leaving the wrong belief unrefuted |
| Drop into the moment | Mission, disaster and discovery stories | No clear "how did we get here" question |
| Bold or contrarian claim | Analysis, "everything you know is wrong" | Needs evidence soon or reads as clickbait |
| Scale shock then meaning | Distances and timescales | A number with no meaning attached |
| Stakes first | Anything with consequences | Fabricated stakes |
| Visual first | Striking assets, muted autoplay | A visual not tied to the message |
| One-line credibility | Contested science, finance, health | More than one line of credentials |
| Roadmap preview | Tutorials, lists | Weakest for story documentary; "is video mein hum dekhenge" as the first line reads as filler |

The best real hooks combine devices (misconception plus puzzle plus a visual). The safe core for a space documentary is one concrete image or scene, one real question, one stake, and a signal of what will be resolved.

Why misconception-first is the strongest-supported pattern: Derek Muller's own research found that viewers holding a firm wrong idea tend to stop attending to a correct explanation. State the wrong belief, show why it fails, then give the right answer (CREATOR). Three of three Hindi reference videos and several Kurzgesagt openings flip a common belief early (OBSERVED).

Curiosity peaks when people know something but not everything: in an fMRI and behavioural study, curiosity about trivia peaked near 50 percent confidence in one's own guess (EXPERIMENT, small student samples). Give the viewer enough known facts to feel what is missing before opening a gap, and use guess-then-reveal: pose, pause, answer.

## Template library (INFERENCE: original syntheses, not performance-ranked)

| # | Template | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Misconception flip | Name the common belief, hint it is wrong |
| 2 | Impossible puzzle | Two true facts that conflict |
| 3 | Drop into the moment | Start inside a tense scene, then ask how it came to this |
| 4 | Scale shock then meaning | A number plus what it means for the viewer |
| 5 | Stakes first | State what is lost if one thing goes wrong |
| 6 | Contrarian with promised evidence | A claim against intuition, with proof promised |
| 7 | Question the viewer cannot answer | A specific comparison where the intuitive answer is wrong |
| 8 | Visual first, then caption it | Show the image, then say what the viewer is looking at |
| 9 | Time promise | Commit to a result inside a stated window |
| 10 | Mystery with a ticking clock | A signal heard once and never again, with every explanation ranked |
| 11 | Reveal the loser | An expectation fails on the last test |
| 12 | Audience-centric problem | A fun fact the viewer was told, with a hidden cost |
| 13 | One-line credibility | One detail every summary left out |
| 14 | Everyone missed it | A famous event, an overlooked decision |
| 15 | Character in a bind | One person, a deadline, an unsolved problem |
| 16 | "What if" scale experiment | A real physical answer to a hypothetical |
| 17 | Negative space | Where we looked and found nothing, and why that is the clue |
| 18 | Counted list with a surprise | "Three ways a star dies, and one leaves something we cannot describe" |
| 19 | Wrong-question reframe | "People ask how big the universe is. Better physicists ask something stranger" |
| 20 | Money shot then rewind | Show the famous image, promise to explain what it is |
| 21 | Absolute negative, then objection | Three short absolutes ("you will never...") then "ok, but how can this be?" (OBSERVED, Kurzgesagt) |
| 22 | Second-person challenge | "You probably think you know X, but you don't" followed by a metaphor for what is hidden (OBSERVED) |

Default picks: 1, 2, 4, 7, 20, 21 for science explainers; 3, 10, 14, 15 for documentary narrative; 6, 12, 19 for opinion or analysis.

## Timeline default (INFERENCE, not validated)

0 to 5 s: a concrete image and a confirming line. 5 to 20 s: deepen the gap and add the stake. 20 to 45 s: narrow the question and promise what will be resolved. 45 to 60 s: hand off into the first chapter with a fresh loop.

## Common mistakes

- Greeting, channel name, "welcome", sponsor read or subscribe ask in the opening. Every vendor source and the 63-graph data agree these hurt.
- Explaining why the topic matters before anything has happened.
- Answering the hook's question in the hook, or swapping in an easier question.
- A number with no anchor.
- Over-promising: "yeh video aapki zindagi badal dega". The payoff must be proportionate to the build.
- Scripting an expectation the facts have not confirmed (the main flaw its own maker named in a Vox black-hole video).
- Metaphors stacked in the hook. One credential at most, in one line.
- "Ye video aapke liye hai" as a standalone line: it announces relevance instead of creating it.

## Hook writing for the ear

Short sentences, the key word near the end of the sentence, one idea per sentence. Put a pause (a full stop or ellipsis) before the turn. Read it aloud against a timer. Pair each line with a visual that adds meaning instead of repeating it.
